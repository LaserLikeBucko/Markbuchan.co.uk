### Article body

For years, the working assumption — inside most organisations, and in most of the advice aimed at burned-out employees — has been that burnout is fundamentally a workload problem. Too many hours. Too many tasks. Too little recovery time. Fix the hours, fix the burnout.

The latest workplace research doesn't support that assumption. Not on its own.

Gallup's *State of the Global Workplace* research for 2025–2026, along with a cluster of related studies from the same period, puts a number on something we've been describing in qualitative terms throughout *The Hidden Gap*: burnout splits roughly 50/50 between the work itself — workload, time pressure, the sheer volume of tasks — and everything sitting underneath it: management quality, fairness, belonging, the actual culture people are operating inside day to day.

That's not a rounding error. That's half.

**The workload half is real — and it's getting worse in a new way**

Unmanageable workload and time pressure remain the single largest driver of burnout, cited by around a third of employees as their primary source of stress. That part isn't new. What is new is where some of that pressure is now coming from: AI.

The assumption going into the last few years was that AI tools would reduce workload. For a meaningful number of frequent AI users, the data shows the opposite. Employees who use AI tools heavily report higher burnout than those who don't — in some studies, 45% versus 35%. Not because AI doesn't work, but because of what using it actually involves in practice: correcting its errors, absorbing the extra tasks it makes newly possible, and doing all of this without any real change to expectations, headcount, or what "done" means. Call it what some researchers are now calling it — AI brain fry. The tool changed. The system around the tool didn't.

That's a workload story with a very 2026 twist. But it's still only half the picture.

**The other half is the one most organisations still don't want to look at**

The people-and-culture side of burnout is where things get uncomfortable, because it's harder to fix with a policy and it points more directly at leadership.

Unclear communication and lack of manager support show up consistently as major contributors. So does unfair treatment — and the size of that effect is worth sitting with: employees who experience unfair treatment at work are more than twice as likely to report high burnout. Not somewhat more likely. Over double.

There's a cascade effect here too. Manager burnout is rising in its own right, and burned-out managers have less capacity to support their teams — which produces more burnout further down the chain. It's a system feeding itself, not a series of unrelated individual struggles.

And belonging matters more than most wellness programmes account for. Employees who feel genuinely connected to their teams report substantially lower stress. Isolation — worse in a lot of remote and hybrid setups — does the opposite.

**Why this matters more than it looks like it does**

None of this is really about burnout statistics for their own sake. It's about where the intervention actually needs to happen.

If burnout is 100% a workload problem, the fix is workload management: fewer hours, better tools, clearer prioritisation. Useful, but partial.

If burnout is also, in equal measure, a people-and-culture problem — fairness, belonging, the gap between what a workplace declares about how it treats people and what it actually does under pressure — then workload fixes alone were never going to be enough. You can perfect someone's calendar and still burn them out, if the deeper signal they're responding to is that the system around them doesn't operate on the values it claims to.

That's the hidden gap, showing up as exhaustion instead of conflict. Same mechanism. Different symptom.

**What actually helps**

Gallup's own conclusion is worth taking seriously: burnout is highly preventable, but the highest-leverage fixes are better work design, manager capability, and genuinely supportive leadership — not wellness perks layered on top of an unchanged system. How people *experience* their workload and their management matters more than the raw number of hours on a timesheet.

That's consistent with what we see in coaching conversations, and — from the clinical and lived-experience side of this work — consistent with what Catherine sees in recovery, too. The way back from burnout isn't purely about resting more. It's about seeing clearly what's actually driving the exhaustion, in the system and in yourself, and closing that gap rather than just pushing through it again.
