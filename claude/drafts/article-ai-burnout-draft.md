**Headline:** Is AI Making Burnout Worse?

**Subhead:** It was supposed to take work off your plate. For a lot of people, it's doing the opposite — and the reason why is a hidden gap, not a technology problem.

**Byline:** Mark Buchan MSc · July 2026

---

### Article body

Every rollout announcement says some version of the same thing: this will free up your time. Fewer repetitive tasks, faster first drafts, more headspace for the work that actually matters. That was the promise.

The 2025–2026 workplace data tells a more complicated story. Employees who use AI tools frequently report *higher* burnout than those who don't — in some studies, 45% versus 35%. Not a small gap. And it's concentrated exactly where you'd expect the promise to have landed hardest: tech, and other fast-adopting knowledge-work environments.

Some researchers have started calling the result "AI brain fry." It's worth understanding what's actually driving it, because the cause isn't the technology. It's a hidden gap.

**What AI actually adds, versus what it removes**

In practice, using AI well is its own kind of work. Someone has to check the output. Correct what's wrong. Decide when to trust it and when not to. And because the tool makes certain things newly possible — a first draft in seconds, an analysis that used to take a day — the natural response in most organisations isn't "let's do the same amount of work in less time." It's "let's do more."

That's not a criticism of the technology. It's what happens when a genuine capability shift meets an organisation that hasn't changed what it expects, resourced, or measured in response. The tool changed. Nothing else did. The gap between "this will make your life easier" and "you're now doing more, faster, with the same support" is a hidden gap in the exact sense the phrase was built for — a declared value (we're doing this for you) quietly running against the operating reality (we're doing this to your output targets).

**Why this lands harder on the people already closest to burnout**

The employees most likely to lean hardest into AI tools tend to be the same people already carrying the most — the reliable ones, the ones who pick up whatever's needed. For a leader or high performer already running a hidden gap of their own — where the values driving their behaviour under pressure don't match what they'd say matters to them — a tool that adds cognitive load while promising to reduce it doesn't relieve the pressure. It compounds it, quietly, in a way that's hard to point to. "I have an AI tool doing half my work" and "I feel more exhausted than ever" don't sound like they should both be true. That contradiction is often exactly what makes people doubt what they're feeling, rather than naming it.

There's a related, more explicit anxiety running alongside this: fear of displacement, or of skill obsolescence, particularly prominent in tech roles where the pace of change has been fastest. That's a different mechanism from cognitive overload, but it compounds the same way — another gap between what's being said publicly ("this tool helps you") and what's genuinely being felt privately ("what does this mean for whether I still have a role").

**What actually helps — and it isn't "use AI less"**

The research points somewhere specific: supportive managers who champion these tools thoughtfully are a real protective factor. Not managers who mandate adoption and step back, but ones who actively shape how a tool gets used — what it's for, what it isn't for, what "done" means now that isn't what "done" meant a year ago, and what gets taken off someone's plate to make room for what's been added.

That's a leadership and culture fix, not a technology fix. It's the same conclusion the wider burnout research keeps landing on: the workload side of burnout matters, but it's roughly half the picture. The other half is whether the people responsible for how work actually happens are closing gaps like this one, or leaving them to compound silently.

If the promise made about a tool and the lived experience of using it have quietly diverged in your team — or in your own week — that gap is worth naming before it gets treated as a personal productivity problem it was never really about.
