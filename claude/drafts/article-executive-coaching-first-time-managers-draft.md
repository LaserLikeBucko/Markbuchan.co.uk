### Article body

Most first-time managers get promoted for being excellent at a job that no longer exists the moment they start the new one. You were good at doing the work. The new role is almost entirely about helping other people do the work — a completely different skill, that nobody actually taught you, that you're expected to have simply because you were good at the last thing.

That gap has a name in management research: the "accidental manager" phenomenon. It's extremely common, and it's not a reflection of your capability. It's a structural gap in how most organisations promote people.

**The peer-to-manager shift is harder than anyone warns you**

Six months ago, the people on your team were your peers. You complained about the same things over lunch. Now you're responsible for their performance, their development, and sometimes their exit from the company. That shift is genuinely disorienting, and most people go through it with no real support — just a new title and an assumption that you'll figure it out.

The specific difficulty isn't usually the tasks. It's the identity adjustment underneath them: you're no longer the person who does the thing well. You're the person responsible for other people doing the thing well, which requires a completely different relationship to control, credit, and what "a good day" even looks like.

**Where the hidden gap shows up for new managers specifically**

New managers commonly fall into one of two patterns, both of which are values-alignment problems in disguise:

The first: overcorrecting into being "the nice manager," avoiding the harder conversations because delivering hard feedback feels like betraying the peer relationships you just came from. This usually isn't a skills gap — it's a values collision between wanting to be liked and needing to hold a standard, unresolved because nobody helped you see it as a choice rather than a personality trait.

The second: overcorrecting into being "the tough manager," proving you deserve the promotion by becoming someone your former peers barely recognise. This is often driven by an unexamined belief that authority has to look a certain way — a value borrowed from a previous boss, not one you actually chose.

Neither is a character flaw. Both are what happens when a values gap opens up under the pressure of a role nobody prepared you for, and gets filled by whatever pattern is easiest to grab under stress rather than what you'd actually choose with a clear head.

**What coaching does here**

Using the VIM (Values Integrity Model) and Inner Coach methodology from *The Hidden Gap*, the work with new managers isn't generic "management skills" training — delegation techniques, feedback frameworks, the usual first-90-days content. It's making the identity shift conscious rather than something you're white-knuckling through alone: what kind of manager do you actually want to be, based on what you actually value, rather than what you're copying from the last manager who happened to be in the room. That's the difference between managers who find their feet within a few months and ones who spend years quietly performing an authority that was never really theirs.
